The number that hides the room

A startup has eleven employees. Ten of them — engineers, designers, support — earn between $35,000 and $95,000 a year. The eleventh is the founder, who pays himself $1.2 million.

A reporter asks: "What's the average salary at this company?" Someone in HR types it into a spreadsheet, hits AVERAGE, and reads back the answer: $163,636.

That number is correct. It is also a lie. Not because the math is wrong — it isn't. The lie is in what the number invites you to picture. You hear "average salary $163,636" and you imagine a room full of people earning around that. There is no such room. There are ten people earning much less, and one person earning vastly more, and the average is sitting in a place where literally no one is standing.

The shift: The mean is not "the typical value." It's a balance point — where a seesaw with a weight at every data point would balance. When the data is symmetric, the balance point is in the middle of the crowd. When it isn't, the balance point can land somewhere no one is standing.

FAQs

If the mean is so misleading, why use it at all?

Because for symmetric data — heights, test scores, blood pressure — the mean and the median agree, and the mean has nicer mathematical properties (it's what the rest of statistics is built on). The mean is misleading specifically when the data is skewed or has outliers. The fix is not to abandon the mean; it's to know when it's lying to you and report a median alongside it.

Is the mean ever the right thing to report for skewed data?

Yes — when you actually care about the total. If you want to know how much money a company spends on salaries in a year, the mean times the headcount gives you the right number. If you want to know what a typical employee earns, the median is honest and the mean is not. The choice depends on the question, not the data.

Why don't news headlines just always use the median?

Because "average" is a more familiar word and "median" feels technical. Most readers don't know the difference. This is why income statistics from serious sources (the Census, the Fed) almost always report median household income — they know which number is honest about what real households look like.

What you now see that you didn't 20 minutes ago

Twenty minutes ago, "average salary" probably meant "what most people earn." Right now, after watching a single founder drag the fulcrum past every employee in the room, you are holding a different picture: the mean is a balance point, and balance points live wherever the weights say they live, including places where no one is standing. The next time you read a headline that says "average household income is $X," you'll know to ask the next question: where is the median, and how far apart are they?

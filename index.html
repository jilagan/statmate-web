The number that hides the room

Eleven people work at a small startup. Ten of them make about $60,000 a year. The eleventh is the founder, who pays herself $1.2 million.

The HR page says: Average salary at this company: $163,636.

That number is not wrong. Nobody lied. The arithmetic is correct. And yet if you took that number to any of the ten employees and said "this is what your company pays people," they would laugh at you. The number describes a room that does not exist. There is no one in the building making $163,636. The closest anyone comes is about $100,000 below it.

This is the kind of number you've been reading your whole life without anyone warning you about it.

The shift: Right now, when you hear an average, you probably picture the typical person — someone in the middle, someone who represents the group. By the end of this lesson you should be holding a different picture: an average is a single point that gets dragged around by whoever is in the room with it. Sometimes the people in the room are bunched up around it and the average is honest. Sometimes one person at the edge of the room drags it five steps away from where everyone actually stands. The average doesn't lie; it just doesn't know how to tell you about the room. Your new job, every time you hear an average, is to ask one question: what does the room look like?

FAQs

Is the average wrong, then? Should I stop using it?

No. The average is a real number with a real meaning — it's the balance point of the data. The problem isn't the average; it's the assumption people layer on top of it that "average means typical." Use averages, but ask what shape they came from. By the end of this unit you'll have two or three more numbers in your toolkit (the median, the spread) that fill in what the average leaves out.

What's the difference between an average and a mean?

They're the same thing. "Average" is the everyday word; "mean" is the textbook word. There are technically other kinds of averages (the median is sometimes called an average, and so is the mode), but when somebody says "the average" without specifying, they almost always mean the mean — the seesaw balance point.

Why does one person drag the mean so much?

Because the mean weighs distance, not count. The founder isn't just one person away from the bunch — she's more than a thousand $1,000 steps away from it. To balance her, the balance point has to slide far enough right that the ten employees' distances on the left add up to her one distance on the right. That happens at $163,636, roughly $100,000 from each employee. We'll come back to this in lesson 3 when we talk about spread — the formal version of "how far from the bunch are people, on average?"

My friend says the median is better. Is the median better?

Sometimes much better, sometimes the same, sometimes worse. Lesson 2 is entirely about when each one tells the truth. The short answer: the median ignores how far the founder is — she's just "one person to the right" — so it doesn't get dragged. That's a feature in some situations and a bug in others.

What you now see that you didn't 20 minutes ago

Twenty minutes ago, "average" probably meant "the typical one." Right now, after dragging eleven figures around a number line and watching one of them yank the balance point a hundred thousand dollars to the right, you are holding a different picture: an average is a balance point, and a balance point moves when weights move, even when most of the people don't.

You can also now do something you probably could not do before: hear a number like "average household income is $X" and feel an honest hesitation. What does that room look like? Is everyone bunched around X, or is X being dragged by a few people way out at the edge? You are not doing math when you ask that. You are doing statistics. The whole rest of this unit is about giving you the other tools you need to actually answer it — starting in lesson 2, with a number that doesn't get dragged at all.
